Suppressive heads across models and tasks 5 studies

Head groups suppress copied tokens, in-context labels and rule answers across several model families. These findings do not establish that the heads share L10H7's mechanism.

  • Selected head groups suppress in-context labels in Qwen3 and Llama; individual Llama effects are small. Andrew Gao et al., 2026 ↗
  • Ablating Pythia canceller heads improves rule-task accuracy; their suppression depends on source values. Han-Yu Wang, 2026 ↗
  • Ablating distinct head groups raises copied-token logits in Llama2-7B-chat, OPT-6.7B and Baichuan-13B-chat. Tao Tan et al., 2024 ↗
Training changes suppressive roles 4 studies

Suppressive roles can migrate between heads during pretraining and change during task fine-tuning. In Qwen3, sums of selected single-head effects suggest stronger braking after instruction tuning.

  • Selected single-head effects sum to stronger word-meaning suppression in Qwen3-4B instruct than base. Andrew Gao et al., 2026 ↗
  • Clean IOI fine-tuning strengthens negative name movers; Subject Duplication changes their suppression target. V. Chhabra et al., 2025 ↗
  • Pythia-160m heads exchange copy-suppression roles during training while IOI metrics remain stable. Curt Tigges et al., 2024 ↗
A head's contribution depends on how it is measured 2 studies

A head's apparent contribution can depend on the task, readout and intervention. Negative direct logit attribution can coexist with a positive patching score, and canceller heads can support antonym answers.

  • GPT-2 Small head 10.7 has negative attribution but positive patching scores on Fair Lending. Ashkan Golgoon et al., 2024 ↗
  • Pythia canceller heads support antonym answers while remaining suppressive on country-capital prompts. Han-Yu Wang, 2026 ↗

Related findings2 groups of studies

Results that bear on this claim without testing it as worded.

Compensation varies across models and training data 5 studies

Later layers can partly compensate for ablated language-model components. Compensation varies with training data and model, and is weak in the tested tabular foundation models.

  • Downstream layers partly compensate for attention-layer ablations in Chinchilla 7B. Tom McGrath et al., 2023 ↗
  • Hierarchical training data strengthens headwise compensation in toy models; the layerwise difference fails statistical correction. Jonas Rohweder et al., 2026 ↗
  • Layer ablations reveal little repair and more redundancy in the tested tabular foundation models. Han Xiao, 2026 ↗
Other sources of compensation 4 studies

Compensation can arise from softmax redistribution and heads that already oppose other components. Final-norm rescaling was small in the tested instruct models, while IOI interaction rankings leave compensation shares unresolved.

  • Standing counterweights appear in GPT-2 Small; final-norm rescaling contributes little in tested instruct models. Areeb Ahmad et al., 2026 ↗
  • IOI copiers interact more strongly with suppressors than backups, without quantifying compensation shares. V. Erramilli, 2026 ↗
  • Softmax redistribution makes ablating similar attention sources together more disruptive than ablating either alone. Joakim Edin et al., 2025 ↗

Context and factual answers

Findings on competition between contextual copies and stored facts clarify when suppressive-head interventions may improve factual answers.

Stored facts compete with contextual copies 3 studies

In GPT-2 and Pythia, boosting suppressive heads can shift outputs toward factual answers. GPT-2 benefits depend strongly on whether context supplies the fact; their role in RAG hallucination remains speculative.

  • Boosting suppressive attention increases factual wins in GPT-2 and Pythia on redefinition prompts. Francesco Ortu et al., 2024 ↗
  • Boosted GPT-2 heads yield more factual answers when the subject contains the fact. Dante Campregher et al., 2025 ↗
  • Attention often targets correct spans during hallucination; copy suppression's causal role remains speculative. ZhongXiang Sun et al., 2024 ↗

Interpreting suppressive effects

When tracing token suppression through MLP layers, check how it depends on upstream attention.

MLP suppression depends on upstream attention 2 studies

MLPs can suppress tokens promoted by upstream attention and amplify attention-based suppression of previous answers. Ablating that attention weakens these effects.

  • Late erasure MLPs suppress promoted tokens less after upstream attention ablation and dominate later compensation. Tom McGrath et al., 2023 ↗
  • Removing attention to previous answers weakens MLP suppression of those answers. T. Yan et al., 2025 ↗

Known gaps in this draft

Open questions concern shared mechanisms across models, compensation shares, factual recall, calibration, individual head roles and dependence on intervention design.

All 7 gaps
  • Suppressive heads have been found across model families, but these studies do not establish L10H7's full query–key and output mechanism. Partial GPT-2 Medium tests and Pythia analyses leave quantitative recovery beyond GPT-2 Small unresolved.
  • The share of self-repair due to copy suppression remains uncertain outside IOI. Group interaction rankings do not measure compensation shares, and other sources include standing counterweights and softmax redistribution.
  • It remains unclear when boosting these heads retrieves stored facts rather than relies on contextual copies. The GPT-2 studies use different prompts, and factual output drops when the context lacks a factual hint.
  • The claim that copy suppression reduces overconfidence rests on limited language-model evidence. The original paper's figure caption and ablation description disagree about the intervention's direction.
  • Joint interventions do not establish each head's role, and Pythia specialization is not established separately for L17H28 and L21H8. Direct attribution and output patching can also assign different signs to the same head.
  • Whether self-repair measured with deletion ablations reflects the same behavior as graded swaps remains unresolved.
  • The Qwen3 base–instruct braking comparison sums selected single-head effects. It does not establish the effect of ablating those heads jointly.
