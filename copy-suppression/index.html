V5Negative heads across tasks and models share the same copy-suppression role 630

Suggested status R2independently reproduced

Several independent groups report behavior resembling copy suppression in other tasks and models. Heads with effects in opposite directions, heads specialized for particular domains, and increased confidence in toy models weaken the claim that these heads all serve the same role.

Original resultMcDougall et al., 2023 ↗

Results that bear on this claim without testing it as worded.

Suppressive heads across models and tasks 5 studies

Groups of attention heads suppress copied tokens, labels supplied in context and answers to rules across several model families. These findings do not establish that the heads share the mechanism of copy-suppression head L10H7.

  • Selected heads jointly suppress in-context labels in Qwen3 and Llama; individual Llama heads have small effects. Andrew Gao et al., 2026 ↗
  • Removing Pythia's output-cancelling heads improves rule-following accuracy; suppression depends on values read from attended tokens. Han-Yu Wang, 2026 ↗
  • Removing distinct head groups raises copied-token scores before softmax in Llama2-7B-chat, OPT-6.7B and Baichuan-13B-chat. Tao Tan et al., 2024 ↗
Training changes suppressive roles 4 studies

Suppressive roles can move between heads during pretraining and change during task fine-tuning. In Qwen3, adding up selected heads' separately measured effects suggests stronger suppression after instruction tuning.

  • Summing selected single-head effects shows stronger suppression of word meanings in Qwen3-4B instruct versus base model. Andrew Gao et al., 2026 ↗
  • Unmodified indirect object identification fine-tuning strengthens heads suppressing correct names; Subject Duplication (repeated subjects) redirects suppression. V. Chhabra et al., 2025 ↗
  • Pythia-160m's copy-suppression roles move between heads during training while indirect object identification metrics remain stable. Curt Tigges et al., 2024 ↗
A head's contribution depends on how it is measured 2 studies

A head's apparent contribution can depend on the task, readout and intervention. Its direct contribution to token scores before softmax (direct logit attribution) can be negative while its score from replacing activations (patching) is positive, and heads that cancel other heads' outputs can support antonym answers.

  • Fair Lending task: GPT-2 Small head 10.7 has negative direct output contributions but positive activation-replacement scores. Ashkan Golgoon et al., 2024 ↗
  • Pythia canceller heads support antonym answers while remaining suppressive on country-capital prompts. Han-Yu Wang, 2026 ↗

Independent groups reproduce L10H7’s suppression of previously seen, predicted tokens on constructed prompts in GPT-2 small. The original study provides evidence that this is its main role across the training distribution, but independent groups have not replicated that finding.

Semantic variants and interacting suppressive heads 3 studies

Copy suppression can target variants with related meanings, and suppressive heads can depend on each other. Toy sorting models can have similar circuits for producing copying and suppression outputs but differ in how their queries and keys determine attention.

  • GPT-2 Small's L10.h7 mediates L11.h10's curved response to the name-mover core. Areeb Ahmad et al., 2026 ↗

V4Copy suppression partly compensates when earlier token-copying heads are ablated 300

Suggested status R2independently reproduced

Independent studies support compensation by other components (“self-repair”) after ablating heads that copy names to the output (“name movers”) in GPT-2 small, with qualitative evidence in Pythia-160m. Evidence is limited to indirect object identification (IOI), which asks a model to identify a sentence’s recipient; independent tests do not isolate copy suppression as the compensating mechanism.

Original resultMcDougall et al., 2023 ↗

The authors ablated three Name Mover Heads, which copy names to the output, in GPT-2 small during indirect object identification (IOI), a task asking for the recipient in a sentence.

Two heads that lower token logits (“Negative Heads”), L10H7 and L11H10, accounted for 39% of the compensation by later components after ablation (“downstream self-repair”). These heads attended more to tokens predicted by Name Mover Heads and decreased those tokens’ logits.

Related findings2 groups of studies

Results that bear on this claim without testing it as worded.

Compensation varies across models and training data 5 studies

Later layers can partly make up for removed language-model components. This compensation varies with training data and model, and is weak in the tested tabular foundation models.

  • Later layers partly make up for removed attention layers in Chinchilla 7B. Tom McGrath et al., 2023 ↗
  • Hierarchical training data strengthens toy models' compensation between heads; the layer-level difference loses significance after correction. Jonas Rohweder et al., 2026 ↗
  • Layer ablations reveal little repair and more redundancy in the tested tabular foundation models. Han Xiao, 2026 ↗
Other sources of compensation 4 studies

Compensation can arise when softmax shifts attention weights between sources, or from heads that already counteract other components. Rescaling by the final normalization layer had a small effect in the tested instruction-tuned models, while rankings of interactions on indirect object identification (IOI) tasks do not establish how much each mechanism contributes.

  • GPT-2 Small heads already counteract others; final-normalization rescaling contributes little in tested instruction-tuned models. Areeb Ahmad et al., 2026 ↗
  • Indirect object identification: copying heads interact more with suppressing than backup heads; compensation shares remain unquantified. V. Erramilli, 2026 ↗
  • Softmax redistributes attention weights, making joint removal of similar sources more disruptive than either removal alone. Joakim Edin et al., 2025 ↗

Context and factual answers

Competition between tokens copied from context and stored facts helps explain when changing suppressive heads may improve factual answers.

Stored facts compete with contextual copies 3 studies

In GPT-2 and Pythia, boosting suppressive heads can shift outputs toward factual answers, with GPT-2's gains depending strongly on whether the prompt supplies the fact. The heads' role in hallucination during retrieval-augmented generation (RAG) remains speculative.

  • Boosting suppressive attention increases factual wins in GPT-2 and Pythia on redefinition prompts. Francesco Ortu et al., 2024 ↗
  • Boosting GPT-2 heads yields more factual answers when the prompt's subject text contains the fact. Dante Campregher et al., 2025 ↗
  • During hallucination, attention often targets correct text; copy suppression's role in causing hallucination remains speculative. ZhongXiang Sun et al., 2024 ↗

Interpreting suppressive effects

Suppression in MLP layers can depend on earlier attention, which matters when tracing where suppression comes from.

MLP suppression depends on upstream attention 2 studies

MLPs can suppress tokens promoted by earlier attention and strengthen attention's suppression of previous answers. Removing that attention weakens both effects.

  • Late token-erasing MLPs provide most later compensation; removing earlier attention weakens their suppression of promoted tokens. Tom McGrath et al., 2023 ↗
  • Removing attention to previous answers weakens MLP suppression of those answers. T. Yan et al., 2025 ↗

Known gaps in this draft

Open questions are whether models share the same mechanism, how much compensation after ablation it explains, when heads recall stored facts, whether confidence better matches accuracy, what individual heads do and how results depend on the intervention.

All 7 gaps
  • Suppressive heads appear across model families, but these studies do not establish L10H7’s full mechanism: how its query–key circuit directs attention and how it changes the output. Partial GPT-2 Medium tests and Pythia analyses leave unresolved how much of a head’s effect this mechanism explains beyond GPT-2 small.
  • Outside indirect object identification (IOI), the share of compensation after ablation (“self-repair”) due to copy suppression remains uncertain. Rankings of interactions between groups do not measure compensation shares, and other sources include components that already oppose the ablated component and redistribution of probabilities by softmax.
  • It remains unclear when boosting these heads retrieves stored facts rather than relies on contextual copies. The GPT-2 studies use different prompts, and factual output drops when the context lacks a factual hint.
  • The claim that copy suppression reduces overconfidence rests on limited language-model evidence. The original paper's figure caption and ablation description disagree about the intervention's direction.
  • Changing multiple heads together does not establish each head’s role, and in Pythia, specialization has not been established separately for heads L17H28 and L21H8. Direct attribution, which estimates a head’s output contribution, and output patching, which replaces its output, can also assign different signs to the same head’s effect.
  • It remains unresolved whether compensation after ablation (“self-repair”) measured by fully removing a component reflects the same behavior as gradually replacing its output (“graded swaps”).
  • The Qwen3 comparison of prediction suppression (“braking”) in base and instruction-tuned models adds up the effects of selected heads tested one at a time. It does not establish the effect of ablating those heads together.
