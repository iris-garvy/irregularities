About the project

Deep learning and interpretability are filled with unexplained phenomena. The irregularities project hopes to be a comprehensive catalogue of these mysteries. Taking inspiration from the field of physics, phenomena become footholds for theories and models to explore. And knowing exactly what the phenomena are is the first step.

Each entry in the irregularities project documents a specific phenomena: the initial experiment that posited its existence, subsequent reproductions, papers that challenge or limit its existence. An irregular entry is meant to be both easy to grasp and comprehensive.

How to use this site

Find an effect
Search for an effect, a claim or a paper, or browse the catalog by topic or open question. Each entry covers one reported effect, such as grokking, double descent or superposition, and the studies collected on it.
Get the key points
Start with "In brief": where the evidence stands and three papers to read first. Then scan the claim rows for each claim's suggested status, a short summary and how many sources support, limit or challenge it.
Weigh the evidence
Open a claim to compare the original paper's result with a representative study from each of Supports, Limits and Challenges, then read the full lists. "Known gaps in this draft" notes what is missing.
Check before you cite
Each study opens to its findings, the measurements and conditions behind them, and a link to the paper, so you can check the source yourself. Every claim and study has its own link you can share.

With an AI assistant

An AI assistant answering from memory or a few search results lacks the evidence compiled here, often from hundreds of studies per effect. Point it at the site so it answers from those records, with links to the studies. 

Start from https://irregularities.org/llms.txt and use the site's records on [effect or claim] to answer: [your question]. Give each relevant claim's suggested status, then the main studies that support, limit or challenge it, with the models, measurements and conditions behind each. Link every claim, study and paper you cite. Note where the records are thin or silent, and keep the site's suggested status separate from your own judgment.

Every page is still a draft: the prose is being rewritten by hand and the AI-generated content checked. Follow the links to the papers before you rely on a result.

How to read an entry

Each entry opens with "In brief": where the evidence stands, and three papers to start with. A reported effect is usually tested in more specific forms than the original paper stated, so the entry splits it into lettered claims: that the effect exists, the settings it needs, how it works, and related effects. Each claim's row gives its suggested status, a short summary and how many sources support, limit and challenge it. Opening a claim shows the original paper's result and measurements, one representative study from each group, and the full Supports, Limits and Challenges lists.

Studies are grouped by what they found for that claim. Each one has a one-line headline and opens to its findings and cited measurements, with a link to the paper. A study appears under every group its findings fall into, so the three counts overlap and are not a score.

Evidence groups

Supports
A finding where the results behave as the claim says, in the setting tested.
Limits
A finding where it holds only partly, only on some models or settings, depends on a choice such as the judge or layer, or costs something it should not.
Challenges
A finding where the effect is absent or reversed where the claim says it should hold.
