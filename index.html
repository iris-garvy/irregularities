A reference for irregularities in deep learning.

About the project

Deep learning research is full of reported regularities: effects one paper observes and later work builds on. Some hold up widely, some only in the setting where they were found, and some do not reproduce.

Each entry takes one such effect, starting from the paper that first reported it, and gathers the studies that test it, with the measurements they report. The aim is a quick, honest answer to how well the effect holds up, and where.

How to read an entry

A reported effect is usually tested in more specific forms than the original paper stated, so each entry splits it into lettered claims: that the effect exists, the settings it needs, how it works, and related effects. Each claim opens to a short summary, a suggested status, the original paper's result, and the studies that bear on it.

Studies are grouped by what they found for that claim. Each one has a one-line headline and opens to its findings and cited measurements, with a link to the paper. A study appears under every group its findings fall into, so the three counts overlap and are not a score.

Evidence groups

Supports
A finding where the results behave as the claim says, in the setting tested.
Limits
A finding where it holds only partly, only on some models or settings, depends on a choice such as the judge or layer, or costs something it should not.
Challenges
A finding where the effect is absent or reversed where the claim says it should hold.

Suggested status

R0claimed in one paper
Reported in one paper, in one setting.
R1one group only
Shown or extended only by the group that first reported it.
R2independently reproduced
Reproduced by a different group, ideally on another model family.
Ccontested
A failed replication in a matching setting, or a credible argument that the effect is an artifact.
?unclear
The evidence collected so far cannot tell.
